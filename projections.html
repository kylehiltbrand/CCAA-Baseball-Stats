2027 Team Projections ADV
Returning production model · CCAA-calibrated

These are returning-production projections at the team level: the model estimates each program's 2026 talent, works out how much of that production graduates, applies a growth curve to the players who come back, and assumes departing production is partially replaced. It reads one season of aggregate output, which is a different and coarser thing from the player model in the Players tab.

The growth factors in step 3 below are still the reasoned values this page launched with. The four-season archive can now measure them directly, and the Players tab does. Reconciling the two is the next job on this page.

Every input is CCAA-calibrated. WAR comes from the same oWAR and pWAR figures used across the site, which recalibrate to league average on every page load. A projection is a central estimate, not a prediction of what will happen.

Methodology
How These Are Built
Five steps · all constants shown
1
Estimate 2026 talent. Each team gets two independent reads: its actual winning percentage (ties count as half a win, NCAA convention) and a WAR-based estimate that starts from a replacement-level team and adds the total oWAR and pWAR its players produced.
WAR win% = .300 + (total WAR × scale ÷ games)
The scale factor is not assumed. It is solved for on every page load so that league-wide WAR reproduces league-wide wins above replacement, which keeps the WAR estimate on the same footing as actual results. Current value: —. The two are blended, then regressed toward .500. Regression is the honest admission that a 20-game high school season is a small sample.
2
Split returning from departing. Seniors leave; freshmen, sophomores and juniors return. Players with no listed class year are treated as departing, which is the conservative assumption.
3
Grow the returners. High school players improve year over year, and younger ones improve fastest. Returning WAR is multiplied by a class-based growth factor: freshmen ×1.35, sophomores ×1.25, juniors ×1.10. These are reasoned starting values, not empirically derived, and are the single most replaceable assumption in the model.
4
Replace what graduated. Programs reload at different rates. The model assumes 45% of departed WAR is replaced, scaled by program strength so stronger programs backfill better.
5
Convert to a record. Projected WAR becomes a winning percentage, blended once more with the regressed 2026 talent estimate, then applied across a season of —, the league median in 2026. A final zero-sum correction shifts every team so the conference averages .500, since the CCAA mostly plays itself and cannot collectively improve.
⚠
Known limitations. There are no park factors, no strength-of-schedule adjustment across the three leagues, no defensive component in WAR, and no accounting for incoming freshmen or transfers. Growth and reload rates are assumed rather than measured. All of these improve once a second season of data exists.

Frozen forecast · CCAA-calibrated
Bands are 20th to 80th percentile
drawn from comparable players

Every returning player in the conference, projected forward one season. Each component of a hitter's line is projected separately and regressed by its own measured reliability, then aged on a curve derived from the archive rather than assumed. This is a different animal from the team model in the other tab, which works off a single season of aggregate production.

These numbers are frozen. They will not move when a stat update is pushed. That is the point: a forecast that quietly rewrites itself cannot be checked, and this one is meant to be checked in June against what actually happens.

Methodology
How the Player Model Works
Six steps · every constant measured
1
Break the line into components. A hitter is not projected as one number. Walk rate, strikeout rate, home run rate, batting average on balls in play and extra-base share are each projected separately, then reassembled into a full stat line. This matters because the components are not equally trustworthy: strikeout rate is half signal at — plate appearances, BABIP not until — balls in play. Averaging them into one figure and regressing them all the same amount throws that away.
2
Weight four seasons. Each prior season is expressed relative to its own league environment, since the CCAA run environment moves year to year, then weighted — from most recent to oldest and by playing time within each season.
rate = Σ(weight × PA × rate/league) ÷ Σ(weight × PA)
3
Regress each component on its own constant. Using the harmonic form n/(n+k), which never reaches full credibility. That is deliberate and it is the opposite of what the leaderboards do: the stats pages describe what a player did, so playing time earns full credit, while a projection estimates what he is, and no high school sample is ever fully convincing.
4
Age the result. Measured from every matched player-season in the archive, pooled rather than averaged per player so a single sophomore who hits three homers cannot write the curve. Shown below in units of league average, after the calibration scalar of —.
Non-seniors return at —, so survivorship is a mild problem here rather than a fatal one.

6
Build bands from comparables, not from a bell curve. Each hitter is matched to the 25 most similar historical player-seasons on strikeout rate, walk rate, power, BABIP, playing time and class. The 20th and 80th percentiles come from what those players actually did the following year. A profile the model has historically struggled with gets a wide band automatically, with no one deciding it should.
⚠
Known limitations. No park factors yet. No incoming freshmen or transfers, so a program's real 2027 roster will be larger than what appears here. Class year stands in for age, which misses anyone who repeated a year. Pitcher projections are weak enough that the scorecard above says so outright. And a player who does not return at all is projected as though he will, because nothing in season totals predicts quitting.
